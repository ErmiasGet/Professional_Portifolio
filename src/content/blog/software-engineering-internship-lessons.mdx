# Lessons From My Software Engineering Internship

During my software engineering internship at a university ICT directorate, I built finance and attendance management systems used by real staff. It was my first taste of enterprise software — and the lessons were not about frameworks.

## What I actually built

- A **finance file management system** with **Admin and Staff** modules, where file access is restricted by role.
- An **attendance management system** with role-based access, organized as separate Admin and Staff pages.

Both were built as full-stack applications, with backend, frontend, and database, and deployed for real use. That alone — real users depending on your code — changes how seriously you take requirements.

## The biggest lesson: requirements are the product

When I started, I focused on code. The internship taught me that the code is the last 20 percent. The people using the system care about:

- Which staff member may open which file — and who must never see it.
- What happens when an attendance record is corrected, and who is allowed to do it.
- How Admin and Staff pages differ, so nobody guesses where a feature lives.

The role-based file boundaries in the finance system came from talking to the people who actually use it — not from my instinct about "standard" permissions.

## Real users break your assumptions

I learned this by watching staff work:

- People did not think in rows of data; they thought in **tasks**: "I need February's file." So the UI had to lead with the task, not the table.
- Users **mistrusted** vague statuses. "It is submitted" was not enough — they wanted to know who had it and when it moved.
- On real data, typos and duplicates stopped being hypothetical. Validation that survived unit tests still needed to be kind to a tired staff member at the end of the day.

That experience is one reason I now design every screen around a workflow rather than a CRUD list.

## Working with a real team and real deployments

The internship was also my first time shipping alongside colleagues with defined tasks, tracked progress, and deadlines that mattered to someone else. Two things made it work:

1. **Clear task ownership** — everyone knew which module was theirs and what "done" meant.
2. **Validate before you call it done** — walk through the workflow as the actual user before release.

## How it changed my projects

Later projects carry these choices with me:

- In **Engida**, the accommodation management system, the workflow comes first: reservation → room assignment → check-in → check-out. The data model grew out of the workflow, not the other way around.
- In **Beten Homes Rent**, the client insisted on role-based visibility so tenants always see their request status ("Waiting for Approval") — the same pattern I first built during the internship.
- I now default to **role-scoped queries on the backend**, because a UI restriction is not a permission system.

## Advice I would give my younger self

- Interview the user before initializing the repo.
- Treat every access rule as a data-layer rule, not a UI rule.
- Ship something real users can touch as early as possible — that is the fastest way to learn what you actually built wrong.
- Keep the task list honest and share it; momentum comes from visible progress.

Internships are usually measured by what you add to a resume. Mine is the reason I design products around people and workflows, and it is why a good portion of my portfolio is business systems rather than demo apps.
