# Building a Newborn Immunization Management System

The project I am most proud to have contributed to is a **newborn immunization management prototype** for a health-sector context in Ethiopia. It is the kind of system that matters beyond screens: missed doses in remote areas are not a UX problem — they are a health problem.

Here is how I approached it, and what the prototype taught me about building software for constraint-rich environments.

## The domain shapes everything

The system targets a network of health centers across **regions, zones and woredas (districts)**, each with health posts and health workers. The users are real officers with specific roles:

- **Region/zone/zonal-level officers** can add, update, view, and export health data.
- **Health-center-level users** manage the day-to-day records for their facility.
- **Woreda officers** can view immunization and pregnancy follow-up data.

That hierarchy means authorization is not "user vs admin" — it is about **which unit the user belongs to** and what data they may touch. A zone officer should not edit another zone's records.

## Automatic schedule generation

The most interesting piece is generating an immunization schedule for each newborn from their birth date. Ethiopia's expanded program on immunization has a known sequence of doses, and the system computes the due dates for each one automatically instead of relying on manually typed entries.

Once a dose is recorded as given, the schedule updates and the next due date is recalculated. This removes a whole class of human error that is easy to make in busy facilities that handle dozens of newborns.

## Designing for unreliable connectivity

Much of the target area has poor or intermittent connectivity. Two decisions mattered here:

1. **A short stack that is easy to run and fix locally.** The prototype uses a **Node.js + React** stack with **MongoDB**, chosen to keep the system light to deploy and maintain in facilities without heavy infrastructure.
2. **Records-first thinking.** The reliable unit of work is a completed immunization record, not a network round trip. Where connectivity allows, data syncs up the hierarchy; when it is offline, the local record still means the child got their dose on time.

## Missed-dose follow-up

A newborn who misses a scheduled dose should not quietly fall out of the system. The prototype includes follow-up logic: when a dose is past due, the responsible health center sees a list of children to follow up on, so the facility can act before a delay compounds.

A real system in this space would also benefit from SMS-based reminders — I deliberately note this as a next step rather than claiming it exists, because building it honestly means not overstating what the prototype does.

## What building it taught me

- **Maintainable and especially runnable beats clever.** In constrained settings, the codebase that can be installed and updated by a local team is the one that survives.
- **Roles must mirror the government/health hierarchy**, with authorization tied to organizational units, not generic levels.
- **Schedule generation is security for the child.** Automating due dates turns a manual, error-prone list into a dependable workflow.
- **Plan for the offline case first**; the connected case takes care of itself.

This project is a prototype at this point. Building it taught me to treat constraint — connectivity, hardware, hierarchy — as a feature requirement rather than an afterthought.
